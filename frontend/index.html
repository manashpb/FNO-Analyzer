    How prices are estimated

    Premium = LTP + Delta × spot move + ½ Gamma × spot move² + Theta / 7, floored at zero. This matches the original calculator. Greeks stay fixed at the refreshed quote; expired contracts use intrinsic value. Estimates are in option points per unit, before costs.

      Understanding Vertical Spreads

      Spread mode compares call and put structures for the same symbol, expiry and two strikes. “Better” means more favorable quoted pricing for equivalent expiry exposure, not a prediction of market direction.

      Two legs per row

      K1 is the lower strike, K2 the higher strike, and W = K2 − K1. Width 1 pairs adjacent available strikes; width 2 skips one strike. Each row shows its actual width. Blue strike pairs contain an ATM leg.

      Call spread: lower-strike call minus higher-strike call. Buying it is a bullish call debit spread; selling it is a bearish call credit spread.

      Put spread: higher-strike put minus lower-strike put. Buying it is a bearish put debit spread; selling it is a bullish put credit spread.

      Column guide

      ColumnMeaning / calculation
      Call BidEstimated credit to sell: call bid K1 − call ask K2.
      Call AskEstimated debit to buy: call ask K1 − call bid K2.
      Put BidEstimated credit to sell: put bid K2 − put ask K1.
      Put AskEstimated debit to buy: put ask K2 − put bid K1.
      Call OppW − Put Bid: compare directly with Call Ask. Lower cost wins for bullish exposure.
      Put OppW − Call Bid: compare directly with Put Ask. Lower cost wins for bearish exposure.
      Strike pairK1 / K2, with the actual width and an ATM-leg marker.

      How to read the interpretation

      At expiry, the long call spread and long put spread payoffs add to W. This lets us compare buying one spread with selling the opposite spread, translating the latter’s credit into a comparable cost.

      • Bullish: compare Call Ask with W − Put Bid. Lower cost wins: “Call debit” means buy the call spread; “Put credit” means sell the put spread.
      • Bearish: compare Put Ask with W − Call Bid. Lower cost wins: “Put debit” means buy the put spread; “Call credit” means sell the call spread.

      Compare Ask with Opp on each side. Opp is red when higher than Ask, green when lower, and yellow when equal (to quote precision) or not comparable. Teal interpretation cells identify the cheaper quoted structure. Neutral cells mean similar cost or unavailable quotes. Calls use rose headers and puts use green headers. These colors identify pricing comparisons; they do not predict direction.

      Example (illustrative, not live data)

      For 1000 / 1050, W = 50. Suppose Call Bid/Ask = 18/20, and Put Bid/Ask = 33/35. Call Opp = 50 − 33 = 17; Put Opp = 50 − 18 = 32.

      Bullish: Call Ask 20 versus 50 − Put Bid 33 = 17 → Put credit better by 3.00 points. Bearish: Put Ask 35 versus 50 − Call Bid 18 = 32 → Call credit better by 3.00 points.

      Pricing limits

      Bid, Ask and Opp always show per-unit points. Enable “Interpretation in ₹ / lot” to multiply the interpretation advantage by the selected symbol’s lot size. For example, 3 points with a lot size of 50 is ₹150 per lot. If lot size is unavailable, the interpretation keeps points and explains why. Comparisons use the loaded snapshot’s bid/ask quotes, before fees, taxes, slippage, financing and margin costs. Both legs need sufficient quote quantity; displayed prices do not guarantee a combined fill.

      Opp uses undiscounted width. Precise put–call parity uses discounted width, so interest and time to expiry can affect the comparison. Missing, zero/nonpositive or crossed leg quotes show — and are not replaced with LTP. Negative synthetic spread quotes can result from wide leg markets. Treat stale or unusual quotes cautiously; a displayed advantage is not proof of arbitrage.
